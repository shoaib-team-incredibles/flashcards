# English Vocabulary

81 words, each with a plain-English meaning, an example sentence, and a reference to help it stick.

## 1. diaphanous

**Meaning:** So thin and light you can almost see through it — think silk, mist, butterfly wings.

**Example:** She wore a diaphanous scarf that fluttered like smoke in the breeze.

**Remember it:** Picture the floating, see-through gowns of the elves in *The Lord of the Rings* films, or a jellyfish drifting in an aquarium.

## 2. peccadillo

**Meaning:** A small, forgivable sin or bad habit — not a crime, just a little naughtiness.

**Example:** Eating dessert before dinner is a peccadillo, not a scandal.

**Remember it:** Politicians' "youthful peccadilloes" is a classic newspaper phrase. Hook: *pecca-* = sin (as in *peccable*), *-illo* = little (Spanish). A 'little sin'.

## 3. pusillanimous

**Meaning:** Cowardly; lacking the nerve to stand up or take a risk.

**Example:** The pusillanimous manager agreed with whoever had spoken last.

**Remember it:** The Cowardly Lion in *The Wizard of Oz* is pusillanimity in a lion suit. Also famous from US VP Spiro Agnew's speeches attacking 'pusillanimous pussyfooting'. Latin *pusillus* (very small) + *animus* (spirit) = tiny-spirited.

## 4. gauche

**Meaning:** Socially awkward and clumsy in manners — says or does the wrong thing at the wrong time.

**Example:** It felt gauche to ask about her salary at the wedding.

**Remember it:** Mr. Collins in *Pride and Prejudice* — his stiff, tone-deaf proposal to Elizabeth is pure gauche. French *gauche* = 'left' (the 'clumsy' hand), opposite of *adroit* (right-handed, skilful).

## 5. obdurate

**Meaning:** Stubbornly refusing to change your mind, even when you should.

**Example:** Despite the evidence, the obdurate juror refused to reconsider.

**Remember it:** Inspector Javert in *Les Misérables* — he'd rather die than change his view of Valjean. Hook: *dur* = hard (like *durable*): a hard head.

## 6. plebeian

**Meaning:** (noun) An ordinary common person; (adj.) common, unrefined, lowbrow.

**Example:** He mocked my plebeian taste for ketchup on steak.

**Remember it:** Ancient Rome split citizens into *patricians* (aristocrats) and *plebeians* (commoners) — think the crowds in *Gladiator*. Short form today: 'pleb'.

## 7. erotomania

**Meaning:** A delusion that someone (often famous or higher-status) is secretly in love with you.

**Example:** His erotomania convinced him the actress was sending him coded messages in interviews.

**Remember it:** Ian McEwan's novel *Enduring Love* — Jed Parry becomes convinced Joe loves him after one brief meeting. Clinical name: de Clérambault's syndrome.

## 8. soirée

**Meaning:** An elegant evening party, usually at someone's home.

**Example:** They hosted a small soirée with jazz, wine and candlelight.

**Remember it:** Gatsby's glittering parties in *The Great Gatsby*. French *soir* = evening.

## 9. perseverate

**Meaning:** To keep repeating a thought, word or action long after the reason for it has passed — getting 'stuck'.

**Example:** After the interview, she perseverated on one awkward answer for days.

**Remember it:** Think of a scratched record skipping on the same line. Don't confuse with *persevere* (keep going toward a goal — good); perseverate is getting stuck in a loop (unhelpful). Common in psychology/neurology.

## 10. palliative

**Meaning:** Something that eases symptoms or pain without fixing the root cause.

**Example:** Lowering prices was a palliative measure; the real problem was the product.

**Remember it:** Palliative care in hospitals focuses on comfort, not cure. Economists often call a quick-fix stimulus 'merely palliative'. Hook: a painkiller, not a cure.

## 11. stymie

**Meaning:** To block or obstruct someone's progress.

**Example:** Red tape stymied the new hospital project for years.

**Remember it:** From old golf: a 'stymie' was when your opponent's ball sat between yours and the hole, blocking your putt.

## 12. railroad

**Meaning:** (verb) To rush or force someone into something, or push a decision through without fair process.

**Example:** The board railroaded the proposal through before anyone could object.

**Remember it:** *To Kill a Mockingbird* — Tom Robinson is railroaded by a biased trial. Image: a train that won't stop or change track.

## 13. punctilious

**Meaning:** Extremely careful about details, rules and correct behaviour.

**Example:** He is punctilious about replying to every email within an hour.

**Remember it:** Stevens the butler in *The Remains of the Day*, or Hermione Granger correcting 'Levi-O-sa'. Your note — 'punctilious prick' — is a someone so rule-obsessed they're unbearable. Hook: *punct* = point (every point exact).

## 14. rudderless

**Meaning:** Without direction or leadership; drifting with no clear aim.

**Example:** After the coach quit, the team looked rudderless all season.

**Remember it:** A ship with no rudder can't steer — it just drifts. Sports writers love 'a rudderless franchise'.

## 15. grating

**Meaning:** Harshly irritating — like a sound or habit that scrapes your nerves.

**Example:** Her grating laugh echoed through the open-plan office.

**Remember it:** Dolores Umbridge's sugary 'hem, hem' cough in *Harry Potter*. Image: a cheese grater on your nerves; nails on a chalkboard.

## 16. plucky

**Meaning:** Brave and spirited, especially when the odds are against you.

**Example:** The plucky underdog team made it to the final.

**Remember it:** Samwise Gamgee in *The Lord of the Rings*, or little orphan Annie. To 'have pluck' = to have guts.

## 17. sapient

**Meaning:** Wise; having deep understanding or intelligence.

**Example:** The sapient old judge saw through the lie instantly.

**Remember it:** *Homo sapiens* literally means 'wise human'. Sci-fi uses 'sapient' for alien species that can think (e.g. *Star Trek* debates about Data).

## 18. dress down

**Meaning:** To scold someone sharply.

**Example:** The sergeant dressed down the recruits for being late.

**Remember it:** Miranda Priestly's icy 'cerulean sweater' speech in *The Devil Wears Prada* — a fashion boss literally dressing someone down.

## 19. indisposed

**Meaning:** (1) Slightly ill; (2) unwilling to do something.

**Example:** The singer was indisposed, so the understudy performed. / I'm indisposed to lend him money again.

**Remember it:** *The Importance of Being Earnest* — Algernon invents a permanently sick friend, 'Bunbury', so he can skip dinners. A very British-polite way to say 'sick' or 'not keen'.

## 20. high-strung

**Meaning:** Very nervous, tense and easily upset.

**Example:** The high-strung host panicked when the soufflé collapsed.

**Remember it:** Monica Geller in *Friends*. Image: a violin string wound too tight — one touch and it snaps.

## 21. squalid

**Meaning:** Very dirty and unpleasant, usually from neglect or poverty; also morally sordid.

**Example:** They lived in a squalid flat with broken windows and no heating.

**Remember it:** Fagin's den in *Oliver Twist*, or the slums in *Slumdog Millionaire*. 'A squalid little affair' = sleazy.

## 22. tawdry

**Meaning:** Cheap, showy and tacky; also sleazy.

**Example:** The casino was full of tawdry gold paint and plastic chandeliers.

**Remember it:** From 'St. Audrey's lace' — cheap lace sold at St. Audrey's fair in England. *St. Audrey* → *t'Audrey* → *tawdry*.

## 23. lackadaisical

**Meaning:** Lazy, careless and lacking enthusiasm.

**Example:** His lackadaisical approach to homework showed in his grades.

**Remember it:** The Dude in *The Big Lebowski* — the patron saint of lackadaisical. From old 'alack-a-day!', a sigh of weary regret.

## 24. frazzled

**Meaning:** Worn out, exhausted and frayed from stress.

**Example:** By the end of the holiday rush, the shop staff were completely frazzled.

**Remember it:** Bridget Jones on any given Monday. Image: a frayed rope or burnt, frizzy hair.

## 25. indefatigable

**Meaning:** Tireless; never giving up or slowing down.

**Example:** The indefatigable nurse worked double shifts all week.

**Remember it:** The Terminator: 'It absolutely will not stop, ever, until you are dead.' — Kyle Reese, *The Terminator* (1984). *In-* (not) + *fatigue* = un-tire-able.

## 26. coup de grâce

**Meaning:** The final blow that finishes something off — often a situation that was already going badly.

**Example:** The rainstorm was the coup de grâce for our already disastrous picnic.

**Remember it:** French for 'stroke of mercy' — originally the shot that ended a badly wounded soldier's suffering. Pronounced *koo-duh-GRAHS* (don't say 'grah').

## 27. détente

**Meaning:** An easing of tension between rivals, especially countries.

**Example:** After years of feuding, the two neighbours reached a sort of détente over the fence.

**Remember it:** The 1970s Cold War thaw between the USA and USSR (Nixon and Brezhnev). French for 'relaxation' — like releasing a crossbow trigger.

## 28. rectitude

**Meaning:** Moral uprightness; always doing what's right.

**Example:** Her rectitude made her the only person everyone trusted.

**Remember it:** Atticus Finch, or Ned Stark in *Game of Thrones* — whose rectitude gets him killed. Hook: *rect* = straight (rectangle): morally straight.

## 29. illustrious

**Meaning:** Famous and highly respected because of great achievements.

**Example:** She had an illustrious career as a surgeon and author.

**Remember it:** Sports commentators: 'an illustrious career'. Hook: *lustre* = shine — a name that shines.

## 30. changeling

**Meaning:** In folklore, a fairy child secretly swapped for a human baby.

**Example:** Villagers whispered that the strange, silent baby was a changeling.

**Remember it:** *Changeling* (2008), Angelina Jolie — police return a boy who isn't her son. Also the 'changeling boy' that Oberon and Titania fight over in *A Midsummer Night's Dream*.

## 31. reconnaissance

**Meaning:** Scouting or exploring ahead to gather information before acting.

**Example:** Before buying the house, we did some reconnaissance on the neighbourhood at night.

**Remember it:** Danny Ocean's crew casing the casino in *Ocean's Eleven*. Military slang: 'recon' (US) or 'recce' (UK).

## 32. geriatric

**Meaning:** Relating to old age or elderly people (can sound rude when used casually).

**Example:** She specialises in geriatric medicine.

**Remember it:** Carl Fredricksen in *Up*. Internet slang: 'geriatric millennial' (born early '80s). Greek *geron* = old man (as in *gerontology*).

## 33. reprobate

**Meaning:** A shameless rogue; someone of bad character, often lovably so.

**Example:** My uncle is a charming old reprobate who still cheats at cards.

**Remember it:** Falstaff in Shakespeare's *Henry IV*, or Captain Jack Sparrow. Originally 'rejected by God' (theology).

## 34. physiognomy

**Meaning:** A person's facial features — or the old pseudoscience of judging character from the face.

**Example:** His physiognomy — sharp nose, narrow eyes — made strangers wary.

**Remember it:** Charles Darwin nearly wasn't taken on HMS *Beagle* because Captain FitzRoy, a physiognomy believer, doubted a man with Darwin's nose had enough energy (Darwin's autobiography).

## 35. skint

**Meaning:** (British slang) Broke; having no money.

**Example:** I can't come out tonight — I'm skint until payday.

**Remember it:** Fatboy Slim's record label was called *Skint Records*. From 'skinned' — cleaned out.

## 36. siesta

**Meaning:** A short afternoon nap, especially after lunch in hot countries.

**Example:** The whole village closes for a siesta at two o'clock.

**Remember it:** Spanish tradition. From Latin *sexta (hora)* — the 'sixth hour' after dawn, i.e. midday.

## 37. capstone

**Meaning:** (1) The top stone of a wall or structure; (2) the crowning achievement of a career.

**Example:** Winning the Nobel Prize was the capstone of her career.

**Remember it:** The floating pyramid tip on the US one-dollar bill. University 'capstone projects' are the final big piece of a degree.

## 38. élan

**Meaning:** Energetic style and confident flair.

**Example:** She delivered the speech with élan and the crowd loved it.

**Remember it:** Fred Astaire dancing up the walls in *Royal Wedding*. French *élan* = momentum, a rush forward.

## 39. neophyte

**Meaning:** A beginner; someone new to a skill, group or belief.

**Example:** As a neophyte in the kitchen, he burnt the pasta.

**Remember it:** Luke Skywalker as a neophyte Jedi on Dagobah. Greek *neo* (new) + *phyton* (plant): 'newly planted'.

## 40. extemporaneous

**Meaning:** Spoken or done without preparation; improvised.

**Example:** Her extemporaneous toast was funnier than any rehearsed speech.

**Remember it:** Robin Williams improvised much of the Genie in *Aladdin*. Latin *ex tempore* = 'out of the moment'.

## 41. accost

**Meaning:** To approach and speak to someone boldly, often aggressively or uninvited.

**Example:** A salesman accosted us the moment we entered the mall.

**Remember it:** Shakespeare's *Twelfth Night*: Sir Toby urges 'Accost, Sir Andrew, accost!' — and Sir Andrew thinks 'Accost' is the lady's name.

## 42. gofer

**Meaning:** A person who runs errands — 'go for this, go for that'.

**Example:** I started as the studio gofer, fetching coffee and scripts.

**Remember it:** Andy Sachs fetching Miranda's coffee in *The Devil Wears Prada*. Pun: 'go for' → gofer (not the animal 'gopher').

## 43. klutz

**Meaning:** A clumsy, awkward person.

**Example:** I'm such a klutz — I tripped over nothing again.

**Remember it:** Mr. Bean, or Bella Swan in *Twilight*. From Yiddish *klots* = a wooden block.

## 44. cloying

**Meaning:** Sickly sweet — too sugary, rich or sentimental, to the point of being annoying.

**Example:** The film's cloying ending ruined an otherwise clever story.

**Remember it:** Critics often call Christmas rom-coms 'cloying'. Hook: like eating a whole jar of frosting — nice at first, then nauseating. Close cousin: *saccharine*.

## 45. verve

**Meaning:** Enthusiasm, energy and vivid spirit.

**Example:** The band played with such verve that the whole crowd was dancing.

**Remember it:** The British band *The Verve* ('Bitter Sweet Symphony'). Similar: élan, gusto, panache.

## 46. philanderer

**Meaning:** A man who has many casual affairs; a womaniser.

**Example:** Everyone knew he was a philanderer except his wife.

**Remember it:** Don Draper in *Mad Men*, or Don Juan. George Bernard Shaw wrote a play called *The Philanderer* (1893).

## 47. sulk pot

**Meaning:** (informal) Someone who sulks a lot — pouting and moody when they don't get their way.

**Example:** Don't be such a sulk pot just because you lost at Monopoly.

**Remember it:** Grumpy from *Snow White*. Image: a little pot simmering with silent resentment.

## 48. headstrong

**Meaning:** Determined to do things your own way and refusing to listen.

**Example:** The headstrong teenager ignored every warning and drove into the storm.

**Remember it:** Merida in *Brave*, or Lydia Bennet running off with Wickham in *Pride and Prejudice*.

## 49. exigency

**Meaning:** An urgent need or emergency demand that forces action.

**Example:** The exigencies of war forced factories to switch to making tanks.

**Remember it:** *Apollo 13* — engineers must fit a square CO₂ filter into a round hole using only what's on board. Pure exigency. Related: *exigent* (urgent).

## 50. sclerotic

**Meaning:** Rigid and unable to adapt or change (originally: hardened tissue).

**Example:** The company's sclerotic bureaucracy missed the shift to online shopping.

**Remember it:** Medical root: arteriosclerosis = hardening of the arteries. Business example: Kodak failing to adapt to digital cameras.

## 51. pro bono

**Meaning:** Done for free, for the public good — especially legal work.

**Example:** The lawyer took the case pro bono because the family couldn't pay.

**Remember it:** Short for Latin *pro bono publico*. Silly memory hook: U2's Bono is famous for charity work — 'pro Bono'.

## 52. half measure

**Meaning:** A weak, partial action that doesn't go far enough.

**Example:** Banning plastic straws alone is a half measure for ocean pollution.

**Remember it:** *Breaking Bad* — Mike's 'half measures' story ends: 'No more half measures, Walter.'

## 53. bucolic

**Meaning:** Charmingly rural and peaceful; idealised countryside life.

**Example:** They retired to a bucolic cottage surrounded by sheep and orchards.

**Remember it:** The Shire in *The Lord of the Rings*. Virgil's pastoral poems are called the *Bucolics*. Greek *boukolos* = cowherd.

## 54. scintilla

**Meaning:** A tiny trace or spark of something.

**Example:** There isn't a scintilla of evidence that he was there.

**Remember it:** Courtroom drama favourite: 'not one scintilla of evidence'. Latin for 'spark' — same root as *scintillating*.

## 55. redolence

**Meaning:** (1) A pleasant, rich smell; (2) the quality of strongly reminding you of something.

**Example:** The redolence of baking bread filled the street. / The town is redolent of 1950s America.

**Remember it:** Proust's madeleine in *In Search of Lost Time* — one bite of cake soaked in tea floods him with childhood memory. Usually seen as 'redolent of…'.

## 56. marinating in memory

**Meaning:** (expression) Letting a memory soak in your mind for a long time, savouring or dwelling on it.

**Example:** I've been marinating in memory all week since I found my old school photos.

**Remember it:** Same Proust madeleine moment: a memory soaking in, like meat in marinade, growing richer the longer it sits.

## 57. parley

**Meaning:** A meeting between enemies to discuss terms; a negotiation.

**Example:** The two gangs agreed to a parley on neutral ground.

**Remember it:** *Pirates of the Caribbean* — Elizabeth Swann invokes the right of 'parley' under the Pirate's Code to avoid being harmed. French *parler* = to talk.

## 58. obsidian

**Meaning:** A glossy black volcanic glass formed when lava cools very quickly.

**Example:** Her hair was as dark and glossy as obsidian.

**Remember it:** *Game of Thrones* 'dragonglass', which kills White Walkers, is obsidian. Ancient people made razor-sharp blades from it.

## 59. skulduggery

**Meaning:** Sneaky, dishonest scheming or trickery.

**Example:** The election was marred by bribery and political skulduggery.

**Remember it:** Derek Landy's book series *Skulduggery Pleasant* (a skeleton detective). Watergate is the classic real-world example.

## 60. disconsolate

**Meaning:** So sad that nothing can comfort you.

**Example:** The disconsolate child sat by the door waiting for the dog that never came home.

**Remember it:** Miss Havisham in *Great Expectations*, stuck forever in her wedding dress. *Dis-* (not) + *consolate* (consoled) = un-consolable.

## 61. promenade

**Meaning:** (verb) To take a leisurely walk in public; (noun) a paved walkway, often by the sea.

**Example:** Couples promenaded along the seafront at sunset.

**Remember it:** Characters promenading through Bath in Jane Austen's *Persuasion* and *Northanger Abbey* — walking to be seen. Brighton's seafront is a classic promenade (Brits say 'the prom').

## 62. suffonsify

**Meaning:** (playful, made-up word) To satisfy your hunger — 'I'm suffonsified' = I'm full.

**Example:** No more pie for me, thanks — I'm thoroughly suffonsified.

**Remember it:** A jokey dinner-table word (blend of *sufficient* + *satisfy*), not in standard dictionaries. Use it with friends, not in an exam.

## 63. ensconce yourself

**Meaning:** To settle yourself somewhere snugly, comfortably or safely.

**Example:** She ensconced herself in the armchair with a blanket and a novel.

**Remember it:** Bilbo Baggins in his cosy armchair at Bag End, or a cat in a sunbeam. Originally *sconce* = a small fort/shelter.

## 64. catch one's drift

**Meaning:** To understand what someone is hinting at without them saying it directly.

**Example:** We may need to 'reduce headcount' — you catch my drift?

**Remember it:** The classic gangster-film line: 'You catch my drift?' Image: catching a current drifting in water.

## 65. browbeat

**Meaning:** To bully or intimidate someone into doing something, often with stern words.

**Example:** He was browbeaten into signing the contract.

**Remember it:** Gunnery Sgt. Hartman in *Full Metal Jacket*. Image: lowering your brow (scowling) and beating someone down with it.

## 66. winsome

**Meaning:** Charming and appealing in a sweet, innocent way.

**Example:** She flashed a winsome smile and the shopkeeper gave her a discount.

**Remember it:** Amélie (Audrey Tautou) in *Amélie*. Hook: someone who 'wins some' hearts easily.

## 67. linchpin

**Meaning:** The person or thing that holds everything together; essential.

**Example:** The goalkeeper is the linchpin of the team's defence.

**Remember it:** Literally the pin through a wheel's axle that stops the wheel falling off. Seth Godin's book *Linchpin: Are You Indispensable?* (2010).

## 68. callipygian

**Meaning:** Having beautifully shaped buttocks.

**Example:** The statue is famous for its callipygian curves.

**Remember it:** The ancient Greek statue *Venus Callipyge* ('Venus of the beautiful buttocks') in Naples. Greek *kallos* (beauty) + *pyge* (buttocks). Modern anthem of the idea: Sir Mix-a-Lot's 'Baby Got Back'.

## 69. rambunctious

**Meaning:** Wild, loud, energetic and hard to control — usually kids or animals.

**Example:** Two rambunctious puppies knocked over the Christmas tree.

**Remember it:** Dennis the Menace, or Calvin in *Calvin and Hobbes*. Synonym: boisterous.

## 70. indigent

**Meaning:** Very poor; unable to afford basics. In law: unable to pay for a lawyer or court fees.

**Example:** The court appointed a public defender for the indigent defendant.

**Remember it:** *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963): the US Supreme Court ruled that indigent defendants must be given a lawyer — dramatised in the film *Gideon's Trumpet*.

## 71. saccharine

**Meaning:** Overly sweet in a fake, sentimental way.

**Example:** The ad's saccharine music made me roll my eyes.

**Remember it:** Named after saccharin, the artificial sweetener — sweet but fake. A cheesy greeting-card verse is saccharine. Cousin of *cloying*.

## 72. treasure trove

**Meaning:** (1) Hidden treasure of unknown ownership; (2) any rich source of valuable things.

**Example:** Grandma's attic was a treasure trove of old letters and photos.

**Remember it:** *Treasure Island*'s buried chest; real life: the Staffordshire Hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold found by a metal detectorist in 2009. 'Wikipedia is a treasure trove of trivia.'

## 73. copacetic

**Meaning:** Completely fine and in good order; no problems.

**Example:** Don't worry about the delay — everything's copacetic.

**Remember it:** Jazz-age American slang; tap dancer Bill 'Bojangles' Robinson is often credited with popularising it (true origin unknown). Very 'cool cat' vibe.

## 74. flat affect

**Meaning:** (clinical) A severe lack of visible emotion — blank face, monotone voice, few gestures. (Often misheard as 'flat affectation'.)

**Example:** After the trauma, the patient showed flat affect, answering every question in the same dull tone.

**Remember it:** Wednesday Addams's deadpan stare is the comic, deliberate version. Clinically it's a symptom (e.g. of depression or schizophrenia), not a style.

## 75. oenophile

**Meaning:** A wine lover and connoisseur. Pronounced EE-nuh-file.

**Example:** As an oenophile, he can name a vintage from one sip.

**Remember it:** Miles in the film *Sideways* (2004), the wine snob who famously refuses to drink Merlot. Greek *oinos* = wine; *-phile* = lover.

## 76. deposed

**Meaning:** (1) Removed from power or high office; (2) (law) gave sworn testimony out of court.

**Example:** The king was deposed in a bloodless coup. / Three witnesses were deposed before the trial.

**Remember it:** Shakespeare's *Richard II* — the king is deposed by Bolingbroke. Legal sense: the endless depositions in TV shows like *Suits* or *Succession*.

## 77. infirmary

**Meaning:** A small medical room or clinic inside a school, prison, camp or base.

**Example:** He spent the night in the school infirmary with a fever.

**Remember it:** Hogwarts' hospital wing run by Madam Pomfrey, or the army hospital in *M\*A\*S\*H*. Root: *infirm* = weak, unwell.

## 78. your reputation precedes you

**Meaning:** People already know about you — your fame (or notoriety) arrived before you did.

**Example:** "Ah, the famous Dr. Ahmed — your reputation precedes you."

**Remember it:** A favourite line in spy and action films when the hero walks into the villain's lair. Can be a compliment or a veiled threat depending on tone.

## 79. avocation

**Meaning:** A hobby or side passion, done for love rather than money.

**Example:** Law is her vocation; birdwatching is her avocation.

**Remember it:** Winston Churchill's painting (he wrote *Painting as a Pastime*), Einstein's violin. Hook: *vocation* = job; *a-vocation* = away from the job.

## 80. malicious compliance

**Meaning:** Following orders exactly to the letter, knowing it will cause a bad or absurd result — to prove a point.

**Example:** Told to 'print every email', he printed 4,000 spam messages: malicious compliance.

**Remember it:** Reddit's r/MaliciousCompliance; unions' 'work-to-rule' protests; and the soldier in the novel *The Good Soldier Švejk*, who causes chaos by obeying every order literally.

## 81. larceny

**Meaning:** The crime of stealing someone's property. 'Grand larceny' = high value; 'petty larceny' = low value.

**Example:** He was charged with grand larceny for stealing a car.

**Remember it:** *Ocean's Eleven* is grand larceny with style. Jean Valjean's stolen loaf of bread in *Les Misérables* is petty larceny that ruins his life.
